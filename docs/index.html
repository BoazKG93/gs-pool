Abstract

Many physical spaces require photographic monitoring of changes to their structure over time. When each visit is reconstructed with 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS), a direct comparison of the two reconstructions cannot answer this because training is stochastic and two reconstructions of an unchanged space never coincide. To address this, we propose GS-Pool, which takes two independently reconstructed Gaussian scans of the same space and returns the changed objects in each, together with their change masks. Our method begins by extracting SAM2 masks from the photographs of each visit. These are then lifted onto the Gaussians that render them and merged into an object pool, a collection of 3D objects, so every decision is taken once per object in 3D. We introduce a novel photographic carrier approach, which co-opts the existing 3DGS mechanism. It does so by applying the photographs of the other visit and back-propagating them to the Gaussians that rendered each pixel. We combine this with GS-Diff's geometry and colour terms and our distilled DINOv3 features. This evidence is compared with objects identified as unchanged in both visits to set a change threshold for each scene.

On the PASLCD benchmark, GS-Pool achieves mIoU/F1 scores of 0.75/0.85 against 0.64/0.76 for GS-Diff, the strongest offline method for detecting change in 3DGS. Finally, we return each changed object as a set of Gaussians, which can be inspected in 3D.

Method

The GS-Pool method. Per-primitive carriers, C/G/P/S for colour, geometry, photographs and semantics, are pooled per 3D object, compared with the other visit's unchanged objects and checked against its photographs, giving the changed primitives and their masks.

Two reconstructions of the same unchanged space never coincide, because 3DGS training is stochastic and each visit is photographed along a different camera path. This difference is termed drift, as in GS-Diff. A difference computed between the two reconstructions therefore contains both actual change and drift. The photographs, unlike the reconstructions, contain no drift. GS-Pool therefore proposes candidate changes from the reconstructions and uses the photographs of the second visit to confirm or reject them. A different object in the same place can remain undetected with geometry and colour alone, so we also distill a semantic field, generated from a foundation model, into the primitives. We keep three components of GS-Diff unchanged: its drift model, its geometry and colour kernels, and its observability weight. We contribute:

  • an object pool: we lift SAM2 segmentation masks of each visit's photographs onto the primitives that render them and merge them across views into objects, so that evidence is aggregated and decided once per object instead of once per primitive;
  • a photographic carrier: we compute the 3DGS training loss of each input reconstruction against the other visit's photographs and backpropagate it through the rasteriser to the primitives that rendered each pixel;
  • a per-scene change threshold: we compare each object's evidence with that of the objects present in both visits, which form an empirical null distribution of unchanged objects;
  • change annotations for the five real scenes of CL-Splats, which has no change masks, following the PASLCD convention.

The photographic carrier

The figure shows why the carrier is needed. A glass emptied in place keeps its shape and its position, so geometry and colour, which compare the two reconstructions with each other, cannot separate it from drift, while the photographic carrier still ranks it near the top.

Because this loss is backpropagated to the primitives, it is aggregated per object and weighed against the other carriers, which a 2D change map cannot do. MV‑3DCD, which compares each view in 2D before fusing, scores 36% below GS-Pool on PASLCD. On its own, the photographic carrier reaches 62% of the full method's score on FastPGSR, and removing it costs 13.0% on FastPGSR and 20.3% on vanilla 3DGS, the largest loss of any carrier.

Every visit is returned as a pool of objects in 3D, each with its decision, its photographic evidence and its changed primitives. Change detection becomes a question about each object, and opens the next one: what kind of change each object underwent.

Limitations

GS-Pool takes all its evidence from the reconstructions and photographs, so a poor capture degrades results. GS-Pool depends on SAM2 for its objects, so a change that SAM2 does not segment as a separate mask cannot be recovered by later stages. A change of lighting alone can still produce false alarms. However, on two captures of an unchanged scene under different lighting, GS-Pool flags fewer pixels than MV‑3DCD and O‑SCD, which we ran from their released code. The distilled field, SAM2 lifting and renders at the other visit's cameras also make GS-Pool costlier in time than comparing the two primitive sets alone. With the distilled fields and SAM2 masks precomputed, one member takes a median 2.1 minutes with FastPGSR and 5.7 minutes with vanilla 3DGS, on one RTX 4090 with 24 GB.
